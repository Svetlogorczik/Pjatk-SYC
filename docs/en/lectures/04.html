Lecture 4

Semiconductors: diodes and transistors

Where "holes" in silicon come from, why a diode conducts in one direction only, and how a small base current controls a large collector current.

📋 Quick summary

✚ = added by the site author — not in the slides or the lecture recording

What a semiconductor is

A semiconductor is a material whose conductivity can be changed over a wide range — by doping, light, temperature. It sits between a metal and an insulator. The most common one is silicon (Si). Semiconductors are used to make diodes, transistors and integrated circuits.

The band model

Electrons in a crystal can only have energies from certain intervals — bands:

  • the valence band — electrons "tied" to their atoms,
  • the conduction band — freed electrons that can move and carry current,
  • the forbidden band (band gap) — energies an electron cannot have; to conduct, it must "jump over" it.

In an insulator the gap is very wide. In a semiconductor it is narrow — at room temperature only a few electrons have enough energy to cross it, so pure silicon behaves almost like an insulator.

🧒In plain words

A ground floor and an upper floor with no stairs. On the ground floor (valence band) it is crowded and nobody moves. Upstairs (conduction band) it is empty and you can run around. The band gap is the height you have to jump. In a metal the two floors touch, in an insulator the upper floor is too high, and in a semiconductor you can make the jump if somebody gives you a boost.

Holes

When an electron jumps to the conduction band it leaves an empty place behind — a hole. The atom is then short of one negative charge, so it is convenient to treat the hole as a positive charge "+" that can move as well.

Intrinsic semiconductor

Perfectly pure, no dopants. The number of free electrons equals the number of holes (n = p), because they appear in pairs. At 0 K there are none; the warmer it gets, the more electron–hole pairs. It conducts poorly.

Doping: n-type and p-type

By adding a tiny amount of another element you can drastically increase the number of free electrons or of holes.

The p-n junction

When p-type and n-type semiconductors touch, something interesting happens at the boundary:

  1. Electrons from the n region diffuse into p, holes from p — into n.
  2. There they meet and cancel out — this is recombination.
  3. Right at the boundary no free carriers are left — a depletion region forms, which does not conduct.
  4. One side is left with excess "+" charge, the other with "−", so a voltage appears — the potential barrier Vbi, which stops further diffusion.

Two directions

  • Forward bias: "+" to the p region, "−" to n. The external voltage overcomes the barrier and the junction conducts — but only once the voltage reaches the barrier value.
  • Reverse bias: "−" to p, "+" to n. The barrier and the depletion region grow, and current does not flow.

Diode

A p-n junction with two leads is a diode — a component that lets current through in one direction only. The lead on the p side is the anode, on the n side the cathode. Current flows from anode to cathode (the way the "arrow" of the symbol points).

🧒In plain words

A diode is a one-way valve in a pipe, or a turnstile: you can go through one way but not the other. And you have to push a little (about 0.6 V) before it moves at all.

The real characteristic is non-linear, so simplifications are used for calculations:

  • Below the threshold voltage VD0 the diode does not conduct (current around 1 nA).
  • For silicon diodes VD0 ≈ 0.6 V.
  • Above the threshold it conducts and the voltage across it barely changes.

✚LEDs and the resistor

A light-emitting diode (LED) is also a p-n junction — it just releases energy as light. Its forward voltage is higher (a red one about 1.8–2 V). It is always connected in series with a resistor that limits the current — without one the LED burns out.

Example: 5 V supply, red LED (2 V), 1 kΩ resistor: I = 5 − 21000 = 3 mA.

The longer lead of an LED is the anode ("+"), the shorter one and the flat side of the case — the cathode ("−"). The same appears in the materials for lab 5.

Bipolar transistor

Three layers of semiconductor: n-p-n or p-n-p. Three terminals: emitter (E), base (B), collector (C). It is called "bipolar" because both electrons and holes take part in conduction.

The key sentence of the lecture: a small current flowing between base and emitter controls a large current flowing between collector and emitter.

  • The arrow in the symbol always marks the emitter and shows the conventional current direction.
  • NPN is more common because electrons are more mobile than holes.
  • ✚ Mnemonic: NPN — "Not Pointing iN" (the arrow points out).

🧒In plain words

A transistor is a tap. The pipe from collector to emitter carries a big stream of water. The base is the handle: a slight turn (a small base current) is enough to let the big stream through or stop it. And the stream is proportional to how far you open the tap — that is amplification.

How to "switch on" an NPN transistor

  • The base–emitter junction must be forward biased: UBE has to exceed the barrier, about 0.6 V.
  • Then a base current IB flows and the electric field "pulls" electrons to the collector — a current IC flows.
  • The ratio of these currents is the current gain:
β = ICIB  (usually above 100)

Characteristics

A transistor is described like a two-port: an input (I1, U1) and an output (I2, U2).

CharacteristicRelationWhat it looks like
inputIB = f(UBE)like a diode characteristic (it is a p-n junction)
outputIC = f(UCE)a family of curves — one for each base current
transferIC = f(IB)a straight line with slope β

The operating point is the specific point on these characteristics at which the transistor is currently working — the pair (IB, UBE) at the input and the pair (IC, UCE) at the output.

✚The transistor as a switch — a preview

In digital electronics a transistor works in two extreme states: cut off (no base current → collector–emitter like an open circuit) or saturated (large base current → collector–emitter almost like a short). The lectures return to this in topic 13, and you do it in practice in lab 5.

Field-effect transistors

JFET (junction FET)

The simplest field-effect transistor. Three electrodes: source (S), drain (D), gate (G). Between source and drain there is a channel; the gate voltage changes its width and therefore its resistance.

  • The gate–channel junction is reverse biased, so practically no current flows through the gate.
  • A bipolar transistor is controlled by a current (base), a field-effect one by a voltage (gate).
  • Input: voltage VGS, output: drain current IDS. Their relation is described by the transconductance:
gm = dIDSdVGS

🧒In plain words

A JFET is a garden hose you squeeze with your hand. You add no water (the gate current is zero) — you only squeeze harder or softer (gate voltage), and less or more water flows through the hose.

MOSFET

Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor FET — a field-effect transistor with an insulated gate (there is an oxide layer between the gate and the channel). Available with an n or a p channel. Most circuits today, especially digital ones, are made in this technology — the CMOS gates from lecture 6 are built from them.

The essentials of this topic — for a fast recap before the lab.

Semiconductors

n-typep-type
Dopantdonor, group V (P, As)acceptor, group III (B, Al)
Majority carrierselectronsholes
  • p-n junction: diffusion → recombination → depletion region → potential barrier.
  • Forward: "+" on p (anode), "−" on n (cathode). Reverse: the other way round — no current.

Diode

  • Conducts from anode to cathode once the voltage exceeds the threshold: silicon ≈ 0.6 V; red LED ≈ 2 V ✚.
  • LED: longer lead = anode (+). Always with a resistor: R = Usupply − ULEDI ✚.

Bipolar transistor

  • Terminals: Emitter, Base, Collector. The arrow = emitter. NPN: arrow out, PNP: arrow in.
  • A small base current controls a large collector current: IC = β · IB, β > 100.
  • It turns on when UBE ≈ 0.6 V (B–E junction forward biased).
  • Characteristics: input IB(UBE), output IC(UCE), transfer IC(IB) — linear.
  • As a switch ✚: no base current → cut off (off); large base current → saturated (on).

Field-effect transistors

  • Terminals: S (source), D (drain), G (gate). Controlled by the gate voltage, gate current ≈ 0.
  • JFET: the gate narrows the channel. gm = dIDSdVGS.
  • MOSFET: gate insulated by oxide; the basis of digital circuits (CMOS).
