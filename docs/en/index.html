How to use this site

🪜Three steps before every class

  1. Read the lecture for the week — each topic is written as normal text, with examples and figures from the slides.
  2. Open the "Quick summary" (the 📋 button at the top of every lecture and in the bottom-right corner) — a cheat sheet with the formulas and rules you need in the lab.
  3. Walk through the lab step by step and do the practice tasks. In class you will get a different variant, but the method is the same.

ℹ️What the coloured boxes mean

  • 🧒 In plain words — the same thing, as simple as possible.
  • 🎓 From the lecture — something the lecturer said live (recording of lecture 1, winter semester 2026/27).
  • ✚ From the site author — my own remarks, examples and tips. This is not in the slides or the recording, so treat it as help, not as "official" content.
  • 📝 Practice task — the tasks are original: same logic as the class tasks, different data. The solution is hidden behind a button.

The course in a nutshell

  • 15 weeks: 15 lectures and 15 labs. Labs run one week behind the lectures.
  • The grade comes from labs only: 14 graded classes × 5 pts = 70 pts. You pass with more than 35 pts. No exam, no mid-term tests.
  • Everything is done in class — no homework (apart from preparing).
  • Labs 1–6: lab equipment (in pairs). Labs 7–11: the Logisim simulator. Labs 12–15: Arduino.

Lectures

  • 1 Lecture 1Introduction, physical basics of electronics and measurementsVoltage, current, Ohm's law, Kirchhoff's laws — and then the key thing for the first labs: how to measure and how to work out how wrong we might be.
  • 2 Lecture 2Passive components: resistor, capacitor, inductor and impedanceThe three basic "building blocks" of electronics and one idea that ties them together — impedance, resistance written as a complex number.
  • 3 Lecture 3Passive electronic circuits: dividers and RC filtersTwo components make a voltage divider, and a divider makes a filter. Plus decibels, the cut-off frequency, and connecting components in series and in parallel.
  • 4 Lecture 4Semiconductors: diodes and transistorsWhere "holes" in silicon come from, why a diode conducts in one direction only, and how a small base current controls a large collector current.
  • 5 Lecture 5Operational amplifiersOne triangle on the schematic, two resistors — and we have an amplifier with exactly the gain we want. Plus the follower, comparator, summing amplifier and active filters.
  • 6 Lecture 6Digital circuits — introduction: signals, gates, Boolean algebraFrom voltage to zeros and ones. What a logic state is, which gates exist, how to write a logic function, and why NAND gates alone are enough to build anything.
  • 7 Lecture 7Minimisation of Boolean functions: Karnaugh mapsThe same function may need ten gates or three. A Karnaugh map is a way to find the shorter version without guessing — and then build it from NAND or NOR gates only.
  • 8 Lecture 8Combinational functional blocks: multiplexer, two's complement, adder, ALUReady-made "bricks" bigger than a gate. The multiplexer as a switch and as a universal logic function, negative numbers in two's complement, the adder and the arithmetic logic unit.
  • 9 Lecture 9Sequential circuits: flip-flops, registers, countersA circuit that remembers. Where memory comes from, how RS, D, JK and T flip-flops differ, what the clock is for, and how a few flip-flops make a shift register or a counter.
  • 10 Lecture 10Designing synchronous sequential circuitsFrom a description in words ("red should light up, then yellow…") to a finished circuit of flip-flops and gates. Four steps: the graph, state coding, excitation functions, output functions.
  • 11 Lecture 11Computer systems, microprocessors and microcontrollersWhat a computer consists of, how a processor executes an instruction, and what a microcontroller is — using the ATmega328, the "heart" of the Arduino UNO board.
  • 12 Lecture 12Communication methods: ports, UART, SPI, I²CHow a microcontroller talks to the world. Serial or parallel, with a clock or without, who starts the conversation — and the three interfaces you will meet in the Arduino labs.
  • 13 Lecture 13Microcontroller peripherals and event handlingHow a microcontroller controls something bigger than itself (PWM, a transistor as a switch, the H-bridge), how it turns a voltage into a number and back — and two ways of reacting to events: polling and interrupts.
  • 14 Lecture 14Computer architecture: the MIPS processor from the insideWe assemble a processor from bricks we already know: registers, memory, multiplexers and an ALU. First the single-cycle version, then the multicycle and the pipelined ones.
  • 15 Lecture 15Assembly language and computer architecture (MIPS)The language a processor really "speaks". Instructions, registers, memory, branches and loops in MIPS assembly — and what such an instruction looks like as 32 zeros and ones.

Labs

  • 1 Lab 1Introductory class: the lab equipmentThe first class carries no points. You get to know the power supply, the multimeter, the generator and the oscilloscope — four devices you will spend the next five weeks with.
  • 2 Lab 2Measuring voltages and currentsThe first graded class (5 pts). You measure voltage, resistance and current — with a digital meter, an oscilloscope and a needle ammeter — and work out the error of every result.

Software you will need

  • Logisim 2.7.1 — the digital circuit simulator used in labs 7–11 (file logisim-generic-2.7.1.jar, needs Java). In the 2026/27 lecture it was said that Logisim also runs in the browser and that there is a newer version, Logisim Evolution (also for macOS) — "very similar, just a bit more complicated".
  • Arduino IDE — for labs 12–15 (Arduino UNO R3 board).
  • ✚ The class .circ files open in Logisim 2.7.1; usually in Logisim Evolution too, but a file saved there may no longer open in the old version — for graded work use the version installed in the lab room.
